The new septic system

How Searenity and Migley ended up sharing one modern septic system, built on the one small corner of land that could hold it.

Background

Why a new septic system?

Both Searenity and Migley had old gravity-feed septic systems: a two-chamber tank draining into a buried leach field. Neither met current building code, but both still passed their inspections, so they were fine to keep using.

The trouble was what might come next:

  • If either system ever failed an inspection (required every 3 years), it would have to be brought up to current code.
  • Any significant renovation of either house would also require bringing everything up to code before a building permit is issued.
  • Septic systems can no longer be located in the shoreline zone, within 150 feet of the high-water mark.
  • Searenity is 100% inside the shoreline zone. It has nowhere suitable for a code-compliant system.
  • Most of Migley is in the shoreline zone too, but there is a small area outside it.

We have also explored building a “Fellow-Shop Hall” on Migley. Knowing the restrictions on where a septic system can go, it felt prudent to get septic sorted out first, keeping future options open for either property.

Step 1 · Summer 2024

Designing the system

We hired septic system engineer Mike Eaton of On Site Septic to come up with a design within these constraints:

  • With no suitable area at Searenity, design one system on Migley, shared by both properties.
  • Septic size is set by the number of bedrooms (not house size, not bathrooms). Searenity needs 3 bedrooms’ worth. Migley needs 4: 3 for the house and 1 for a possible Fellow-Shop Hall. So the design is for a 7-bedroom system.
  • Soil permeability tests were done in the part of Migley outside the shoreline zone. Today’s code requires much higher permeability for a gravity drain field than it used to.

The soil tests showed the area wasn’t suitable for a drain field. Instead it would need a Glendon Bio-filter: rather than a buried field, a mound is built with technology inside it that treats the sewage. The bio-filter mound has to be:

  • outside the shoreline zone,
  • 5 feet back from property lines,
  • 100 feet from any well whose casing is less than 20 feet deep,
  • and big. A 7-bedroom system needs a sizeable mound area.

The problem

There wasn’t enough of Migley outside the shoreline zone to meet all of that. It looked like we couldn’t make a workable design, and asking a neighbor for an easement to put the mounds on their land was starting to look like the only option.

The solution

  • Mike asked the Whatcom County Health Department for a variance on the 100-foot setback from the Migley well. That well’s casing is 17 feet deep, just 3 feet short of the 20 feet that allows a smaller setback.
  • The Health Department granted it, requiring only a 75-foot setback from the Migley well, based on the rock structure around the well and on condition that the system is inspected every year. The 100-foot setback from our neighbor’s well stayed at 100 feet.
  • A legal easement was created between Migley and Searenity, protecting Searenity’s right to use and maintain the shared system no matter who owns the properties in future. The county required this before issuing the permit.
  • With that, the permit was issued.

Step 2 · Fall 2025

Clearing the trees

Twelve trees stood where the bio-filters would go. We hired Dan Dittrich, and over two days he and his son Robin cut them down, bucked the trunks into 15-foot lengths for future firewood, and chipped all the limbs.

About half the stumps could stay in the ground, so those were cut flush. The rest had to come out for the bio-filters. Those were left about 6 feet tall so the septic installer could pull them out with his bigger excavator during the build.

Step 3

The build begins

The permit is good for 3 years. We felt it was wise to go ahead before it expired, since regulations may get stricter and there’s no guarantee the variance would be granted again.

We chose Rich and Britta Hudson, owners and operators of Island Excavation, for the job. Their business is right here on Lummi Island and they do most of the septic installations on the island. Rich is licensed and a certified Glendon Bio-filter installer, and they have a very good reputation for septic work here.

First, before the old tanks could be decommissioned, Baker Septic pumped them out.

Step 4

Searenity’s new tank

A typical septic tank has two chambers. Raw sewage flows into the first, where solids settle to the bottom, and the liquid flows on to the second. More solids settle out there, and the liquid then usually leaves the tank for the drain field or filter.

At Searenity, though, the liquid has to get to the dosing pump tank, which is a few hundred feet away and uphill. So we used a combination septic and pump tank with a third chamber, essentially a sump, fitted with a level sensor and a pump.

Taking out the fence between Searenity and Migley was not too hard. All the posts were rotten!

The tank and its lid together are too heavy to lift at once, so the lid was set aside first.

Next, the house’s sewer line was connected to the tank.

An electrical control box went on Searenity’s outside wall, wired to the level sensors and pump in the third chamber. It includes an alarm: if the level in the tank gets too high (say, from a pump failure), a horn sounds. The box needs two feeds on two separate breakers, so the alarm still works even if the pump fails or its breaker trips.

Then the old tank was decommissioned: lids and risers removed, and the tank filled with sand.

Perhaps of interest: when the house’s outlet pipe was connected to the new tank, we found that only the bathroom (toilet, sink and shower) drains to the tank. Where the kitchen sink goes is still a bit of a mystery!

Step 5

Migley’s new tank

Migley’s tank can drain to the pump tank by gravity, so a normal two-chamber tank does the job. The old tank was broken up and removed, and its pit was enlarged for the new one.

Interestingly, three pipes ran into the old tank: an old metal pipe from the kitchen drains, a newer ABS plastic pipe from the bathroom drains, and the downspout drains. Only the ABS pipe was connected to the new tank. Plumbing work in the Migley crawlspace tied the kitchen drains into that same ABS pipe, and the downspouts now go to a separate surface drain.

Step 6

The dosing pump tank

The design called for a 2,500-gallon pump tank. Think of it as the system’s capacitor. Sewage leaving both septic tanks is stored here and pumped out at a slow, controlled rate. When a lot is coming in, the tank fills; when little is, it’s slowly pumped down. That keeps the flow into the bio-filters steady.

A 2,500-gallon tank is too big and heavy to transport, so two 1,250-gallon tanks were installed and connected to work as one.

Steps 7 and 8

Connecting it all, and power

First, the gravity line from Migley’s septic tank to the pump tank. Then a pressure line from Searenity’s tank to the pump tank.

A control panel went on the outside of the Migley garage, wired to the level sensor and pump in the pump tank. It has an alarm, a horn and a light, if the level gets too high. Like Searenity’s, it’s on two separate breakers, so if the pump circuit fails the alarm still works.

Step 9

Building the bio-filters

Because of the space constraints, the bio-filter had to be built as two filters rather than one big one. This is where those twelve trees used to be.

The ground is scraped level and a treated wood frame is built.

The edges are smoothed, the frame filled with sand, and the sand watered down to settle it, then leveled.

A waterproof liner goes in, with perforated pipe.

Then it’s filled with Glendon’s proprietary plastic pellets.

That’s covered with fine mesh netting and then large-bead gravel. Another layer of netting goes over the gravel, and then sand.

The pump tank is connected to the bio-filters, with the flow split between the two through a manifold.

Then the mounds are shaped and finished.

Finally, a straw blanket protects the mounds from erosion until the vegetation takes hold.

Step 10

Finishing up

Rebuild the fence, smooth the yard, and get it ready for grass seed.

Where everything ended up

Searenity: 3 lids for the 3-chamber tank, and the control panel on the house wall behind the “Mary Lee Maple”!

Migley: 2 lids for the 2-chamber tank, under the wine barrels. You’ll notice a neat little line in the dirt between Searenity and Migley. That’s where we re-buried the signal wire, about 2 inches deep, that helps our robot mower find its way back to its charging station. It was taken out at the start of the project.

Pump tank: the 4 lids of the 2,500-gallon pump tank, on the water side of the Migley garage.

Bio-filters: the two mounds together give the bio-filter enough area to process the sewage of a 7-bedroom home.

Finally

Searenity and Migley now have a septic system that will let either or both properties be renovated, expanded, or rebuilt.

But for now, all this for 2 toilets!

Bowel movements are now soooo much better.
