Section 1

Background

The existing fence was installed before we bought Searenity in 2015. It wasn’t in the best of shape, but it was straight and solid. It was also brown!

Within the first year, Jim painted the fence light blue to match the house.

It was beginning to fail in places. The driveway gate was rotten and would not open and close properly, and it kept on decaying. Rotten fence boards would blow out in high winds; they were put back with baler twine and duct tape!

The header boards were sagging. And a large cedar tree had grown to the point where it had lifted the fence and pushed it toward the road.

Posts were rotting and breaking in the wind, with entire sections falling.

It was time to rebuild the fence!

Section 2

Decision

In June 2026, Trish, Verna, Sharon and Dave met at Searenity. We discussed the fence project and agreed to proceed. The objectives of the fence project were to build a fence that would:

  • appropriately adorn Searenity, one of the most awesome properties in the entire San Juan Islands.
  • provide a visual barrier from the road. As cars come around the hard hairpin turn where N Nugent and W Shore meet, their headlights shine directly into the Searenity windows (Searenity is a bit of a fish bowl).
  • withstand the high winds of winter storms.
  • be reasonably maintenance-free.

Our good friend and design/construction partner Steve Thomas was his normal talented self and had lots of good ideas and input. Conceptually, he encouraged us to look at a horizontal picket fence. His neighbor built one, and Steve recently built one for himself. The features of the design:

  • It creates a visual barrier.
  • It leaves openings for the wind to pass through the fence.
  • The horizontal pickets support themselves. There is no need for a support structure between the posts to hold up the pickets (and it is this support structure that often sags over time).
  • It has a clean, attractive look.

Section 3 · August 17 and 18

Demolition

Removing the old fence was a joyous day. It was nice to remove the albatross it had become. First we recorded the height of the existing fence (7′ 10″).

Some pickets were salvageable. We kept a few, but most were taken for future project use by Oscar and Charlie.

Posts were removed. Although many bottoms were rotten, most of the above-ground portion was in excellent condition. We saved these 4×6 treated wood posts for future projects.

There was a 1-foot retaining wall made of bricks on the outside of the fence. We removed it and did not replace it in the new design.

A buried utility locate was done. The buried phone line was the biggest concern before digging new post holes.

Section 4 · August 19 to 21

Post anchors

The relevant criteria for the post anchors were:

  • Spaced approximately 6 feet apart (center to center). We purchased 6-foot and 8-foot stringers: 6-foot where the post spacing was less than 6 feet, and 8-foot where it was more than 6 feet.
  • We did not want to use treated wood posts, since they would be visible in the fence design. So the posts are 4×4 Douglas fir. The only issue is that Douglas fir will rot if buried in soil. So metal anchors were cemented in the ground and the posts were connected to the anchors, keeping the wood completely out of the ground.
  • We had the complexity of the large cedar tree close to the road. We explored a number of options, but decided to have the fence butt up to the tree on both sides, integrating the tree as part of the fence wall.
  • The first step was to bury 10-inch diameter concrete form tubes 2.5 feet in the ground. We hand-dug the holes, positioned the tubes, and filled around them with compacted soil.
  • We stretched a line the length of the fence and, at 6-foot intervals, hung a plumb bob from the string to find the center of each post location.
  • The 6-foot interval was approximate, given some rocks that needed to be considered, the tree that needed to be worked with, and the gate in the middle of the fence for access from the road to the yard, particularly for propane tank filling. We wanted this gate in the same position as the previous gate, as there were landscape features that worked with this location. The critical part was that the posts were all precisely in line with each other; the distance between posts could vary (within reason).
  • The majority of the fence is straight and parallel with the road. However, the 6 posts closest to the garage gently arc as the road begins to turn. We used a jig made of 8-foot 2×2s to simulate the 6-foot picket lengths in an arc pattern, to locate the positions of the posts within the arc.
  • The tubes needed to be close (within 1 to 2 inches) to “perfect.” The positioning of the post bracket inside the tube when the concrete was added needed to be “perfect.”

Time for concrete and post brackets!

Concrete-set fence post brackets are commercially available. But this fence will be subject to strong 60 to 80 mph winds, and the available brackets did not clamp very high up the sides of the posts (less than 1 foot). This did not seem enough, particularly given the 6.5-foot-high posts and fence wall we were building. So we designed our own, and George’s business, Industrial Fabrication Co., drafted and fabricated 32 of them for us.

These were made from stainless steel to resist rust, given the salt spray from the ocean. The two bolt holes are ¾ inch. We used ⅝-inch stainless bolts to attach the posts.

Reinforcement bar (rebar) went into each hole, surrounding the post stand. The rebar was cut and shaped for us at Carlson Steel in Bellingham.

We temporarily attached 2-foot lengths of old 4×4 into each post bracket. These enabled us to:

  • Manipulate the post bracket in the wet concrete.
  • Center the post bracket plumb under the fence line string.
  • Use a post level to orient the post bracket.
  • Secure the post bracket while the concrete cured.
  • Avoid stressing the concrete while it cured.

And then we let the concrete cure for two weeks.

Section 7 · September 6 to 10

Top rail

We added a decorative top rail across the entire fence. We chose Iron Wood (sometimes called Epay) due to its hardness and strength. It is a 1-inch thick layer, on its flat side, and the expectation is it will not sag.

Iron Wood is a tropical hardwood from Central and South America. It is so dense that it sinks in water, and it naturally resists rot and insects, so it can last for decades outdoors without treatment. Left unfinished, it weathers to a silver-grey.

Section 8 · September 8 to 12

Gates

There are three gates needed:

  • A double door gate, wide enough to drive a vehicle through to the north side of the garage. This was made to look identical to the fence.
  • A single door gate toward the south side of the fence as an access primarily for having the propane tank filled. This was made to look identical to the fence.
  • A main entrance gate at the top of the ramp. This gate is being metal fabricated by Duncan McLean, a “metal artist” who lives on Lummi and has made other artistic gates on the island. This will be completed in early 2027.

To build the “fence like gates” we had George and Industrial Fabrication Co fabricate custom fence brackets (pic below) that would hold 2×4 frame pieces together, made from stainless steel, and include an anchor point for the adjustable strength cable.
